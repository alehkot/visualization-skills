# Illustration review

Use this reference for explanations involving physical parts, spatial structure, motion, reconstruction, or analogy. Review the claimed relationships and the disclosed drawing conventions; photorealism and stylistic preference are not correctness tests.

## Establish what the depiction claims

Read the title, labels, legend, caption, and surrounding explanation before interpreting apparent geometry. Determine whether the view claims to be measured, schematic, sectioned, exploded, reconstructed, or illustrative. A conventional transformation can reveal structure without claiming literal appearance.

Identify the source-supported parts and relationships relevant to the reader's task. Inspect adjacency, containment, order, connection, contact, orientation, or motion as needed. Do not infer exact dimensions from an explicitly schematic view unless the artifact separately gives them as measured quantities.

Source absence is not proof that an unseen interior feature is fabricated. If underlying structure is unavailable, leave its fidelity unverified. A supplied source that explicitly contradicts the depicted connection can establish a finding.

## Inspect the transformation, not just the transformed shape

| Depiction | Check | Avoid mistaking convention for error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cutaway or section | Removed surfaces, retained context, identity of exposed parts | Missing visible material may be intentionally removed for explanation. |
| Exploded view | Part correspondence, assembled order, actual versus displaced position | Gaps need not represent operating clearances. |
| Transparent overlay | Whether surfaces and connections can be distinguished | Transparency can be conceptual rather than a material property. |
| Magnified inset | What region is enlarged and how it corresponds to the main view | A larger inset part need not imply a larger real part. |
| Motion sequence | Stable identity, viewpoint, direction, and stated timing | Selected frames need not depict equally spaced moments. |
| Reconstruction | Observed, restored, inferred, and unknown portions | A hypothetical restoration can be legitimate when clearly identified. |

Disclosure does not excuse every mismatch. “Not to scale” does not repair an incorrect connection or reversed layer order. “Simplified” does not authorize adding a source-contradicting part. Establish which relationship the task relies on before assigning materiality.

## Distinguish arrows and paths

Follow a leader to the part it labels. Distinguish that leader from motion, force, fluid flow, assembly direction, or a causal link. Check that the relevant direction and endpoint agree with the stated operation.

An arrow's intended role may be clear from a nearby label or consistent convention. Do not require different styles for every role if meaning is already recoverable. If two incompatible roles remain indistinguishable at a consequential junction, identify that junction and suggest a label or another specific disambiguation.

When comparing frames, separate object motion from camera movement. A rotated view can look like a rotated part if no correspondence is available. But different viewpoints alone do not establish a defect when labels and landmarks make them explicit.

## Worked example: surviving versus reconstructed material

An invented museum source says that a vessel's base and one handle survive, while the neck and rim are reconstructed. The explanation's stated purpose is to show visitors which parts are original.

A uniform finished drawing with no restoration labels or equivalent distinction fails that purpose: it gives no way to recover the original/reconstructed boundary. A minimal repair is to identify the surviving base and handle and distinguish the reconstructed neck and rim through direct labels or a defined visual treatment. Do not invent the exact missing contour if the source does not specify it.

A control version clearly labels those parts and explains the reconstruction in its caption. It need not use a particular dashed line or show an unattractive unfinished surface. Uniform shading alone is no longer a material defect if the text supplies the required distinction clearly.

If the task were simply to discuss the vessel's proposed overall silhouette, the same restoration could be appropriate with its hypothetical status stated. The reader question changes the required distinctions.

## Check analogies and suggest bounded repairs

For a visual analogy, identify the relationship transferred to the subject and any extra implication the image introduces. A branching tree may communicate descent while incorrectly suggesting exclusive parentage. Report that implication only when it conflicts with the subject or blocks the requested reading; analogies need not match every incidental feature.

Prefer repairs to the affected label, leader, orientation, visibility, correspondence, or qualification. A new viewpoint is useful if it exposes a necessary relationship that the current view hides. Request source geometry when a factual correction depends on it instead of supplying a plausible but unverified reconstruction.

Separate fidelity from production quality. A low-resolution view can prevent inspection without proving the original is illegible. A mechanically accurate drawing can still hide its key contact point. Describe the evidence for the particular finding.

## Basis and limits

- [Agrawala, Li, and Berthouzoz, Design Principles for Visual Communication](https://sites.astro.caltech.edu/~george/aybi199/ACM_Viz.pdf) examines technical-illustration conventions and their domain-specific purposes. Those conventions motivate checks, not universal verdicts.
- [Tversky, Visualizing Thought](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2010.01113.x) discusses how visual marks and spatial arrangement affect expressed meaning.

The vessel example and review procedure are original guidance. Neither an accurate-looking image nor a passed checklist establishes measured reader understanding.
