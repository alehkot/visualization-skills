# Explanatory illustration

Use this reference when physical structure, spatial relationships, motion, or a visual analogy carries the explanation. Choose what must be visible before choosing an artistic treatment. This is guidance for the brief, not permission to generate or edit images beyond the user's request.

## Separate known structure from representational choices

Extract the relevant parts, their connections, relative positions, and actions from the source. Distinguish measured geometry, qualitative spatial statements, inferred structure, and unknowns. Photographic realism can make an unsupported detail look observed; do not fill source gaps with plausible-looking anatomy or machinery.

Decide which relationships must survive simplification: adjacency, containment, contact, separation, orientation, ordering of layers, or a path through the object. Preserve the relationships that answer the question even when exact dimensions are unnecessary.

Name the operation the reader needs to perform: identify a part, trace a force or flow, understand assembly, compare two states, or distinguish observed material from reconstruction. A different operation may require a different viewpoint or second panel.

## Select a visibility strategy

| Strategy | What it reveals | What the brief must preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exterior annotated view | Part identity, location, orientation | Labels associated with the correct visible part; relevant landmarks. |
| Cutaway or section | Hidden structure or interior relationships | Where material is removed, remaining shape, and what the section represents. |
| Exploded view | Assembly order and relationships between parts | Correspondence to the assembled object; displacement distinguished from actual spacing. |
| Transparent overlay | Interior and exterior context together | Which surfaces are conceptual overlays, with enough distinction to trace the subject. |
| Sequence of states | Movement, deformation, or operation | Stable part identity and orientation; what changes between frames. |
| Schematic diagram | Functional connection independent of exact geometry | Which connections are physical, causal, or merely explanatory. |

Select the least transformation that exposes the needed relation. A cutaway that removes the only landmark for orientation may need a small exterior view. An exploded view can clarify assembly while making actual operating position harder to infer. These are tradeoffs, not reasons to prohibit either convention.

Do not invent the hidden structure merely because a cutaway would be appealing. If the source supports function but not geometry, use a functional schematic and identify that limit.

## Specify motion, scale, and reconstruction

Disambiguate motion arrows from label leaders, flow paths, forces, and assembly directions where those coexist. A rotational arrow needs a recognizable rotating part or axis; a straight arrow can otherwise imply translation. Do not add an arrow to every part by convention.

When frames show selected states, avoid implying known durations between them unless timing is supplied. Maintain identity through changed orientation, and label a viewpoint change when it could look like object motion.

Disclose changes of scale when they affect the intended reading. An inset can magnify a small contact point while a main view preserves overall structure. A scale qualification does not excuse reversing the order of layers or showing disconnected parts as touching.

Separate surviving or measured features from reconstructed or hypothetical ones when that distinction matters. A legend, direct annotation, or differentiated treatment can supply the distinction; no fixed line style is mandatory.

For an analogy, state the actual correspondence and a limit that matters. Calling a process a “conveyor belt” may explain order but incorrectly suggest fixed speed, no branching, or constant capacity. Prefer a literal relationship diagram if the analogy's implied claims overwhelm its explanatory benefit.

## Worked example: explain a music-box mechanism

An invented description says that turning a handle rotates a pinned cylinder, whose pins lift and release the teeth of a metal comb. It supplies no dimensions, gear layout, or operating speed.

A useful brief proposes a schematic interior view showing handle, cylinder, and comb, with the cylinder-to-comb contact visible. If the housing obscures that relation, identify it as removed for explanation. Label the rotation and the lifting/release action separately; use a second state only if it clarifies the release.

Specify “schematic arrangement; dimensions and drive linkage not supplied.” Do not draw an invented gear train as a known component or show a timed trajectory. If the task is only to identify the outside controls, an exterior labeled view is sufficient and the cutaway adds unnecessary detail.

## Handoff checks

List the supported parts, the view or sequence, the important relationship each reveals, and any altered visibility, scale, or placement. Keep essential captions attached to the relevant view. Ask whether the intended reader could distinguish real structure from the explanatory transformation without having to guess.

## Basis and limits

- [Agrawala, Li, and Berthouzoz, Design Principles for Visual Communication](https://sites.astro.caltech.edu/~george/aybi199/ACM_Viz.pdf) examines domain-specific principles, including technical illustration and maps. Its examples motivate visibility and spatial reasoning; they do not validate every proposed cutaway or analogy.
- [Tversky, Visualizing Thought](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2010.01113.x) discusses the meanings carried by marks, arrows, and spatial arrangements.

The procedures and music-box example are original adaptations. They support a faithful brief; finished visual clarity and reader understanding require their own inspection or study.
